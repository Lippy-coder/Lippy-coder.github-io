# Glendale Forecasts From Space, Alerts From Ground

Glendale's app works in two layers, and only one of them can be copied. The first layer is an **IMF screen built from NOAA/SWPC satellite data**. The developer describes it as a **30-minute window "currently arriving at Earth", with a window on either side**, a percentage that shows "how negative the Bz was" (100% is ideal) and a 24-hour plot of the clock angle, theta. He uses this layer to look **1 to 3 hours ahead** ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/)). The second layer drives the alerts: a **proprietary "substorm strength" number in nT, built from ground magnetometers** in Norway, Sweden, Finland, the UK, the US and Canada. Push alerts escalate from **onset to yellow, amber, red, major, severe and extreme**, and they are sent only when it is dark where you are ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/); [glendaleskye.com](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-alerts-app.php)). No source gives the nT thresholds or the index formula, and a 2026 academic review confirms the method has not been published ([Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2026.1749567/xml)). Aurora Dash already does most of what the IMF layer does. The main changes needed are to **shorten its windows from 60 to 30 minutes, fetch enough history to fill them, and add an alert rule with hysteresis and a darkness check**. The magnetometer layer can only be approximated with public substitutes such as NOAA's 1-minute estimated Kp, OVATION or a textbook substorm-onset test. **One limitation applies to the whole report: none of the source pages (Glendale's own site, the press articles, the guides or NOAA's documentation) could be opened in full. Every finding comes from search-engine snippets of those pages.**

## Glendale watches the sky in space, then confirms on the ground

**Documented.** Stables is a crofter, web developer and aurora photographer in Glendale on Skye. He says he has done daily research there since September 2012 ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/); [Flickr](https://www.flickr.com/people/glendaleskye/)). A 2017 news report says he spent about a year photographing substorms and matching the photos against satellite and magnetometer data ([Press and Journal](https://www.pressandjournal.co.uk/fp/news/1140162/skye-crofter-creates-viral-aurora-app/)). He credits **DSCOVR, ACE and SDO satellite data and NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center** for the solar wind. For the live ground data he credits **the Tromsø Geophysical Observatory, the Swedish Institute of Space Physics, USGS, the Finnish Meteorological Institute, the British Geological Survey and Natural Resources Canada** ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/)). He calls Kp "totally unreliable" and avoids "unreliable Kp or ovation model data" ([faqs.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/faqs.php); [glendaleskye.com](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-alerts-app.php)). He says Norwegian magnetometers match visible aurora over Scotland better than UK ones, which "fail to detect activity or react a couple of hours after the light show started" ([faqs.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/faqs.php)).

The headline number on the app is **"substorm strength (aka Auroral Substorm Index)"**, a negative value in nT that is "the strength of the aurora in the sky right now". The app calls it "the single most important number on the app and overrides all others" ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/)). Each substorm is labelled with a phase: growth (charging), expansion (energy being released as aurora) and recovery (fading). On his photo guide, Stables links expansion to the moment the magnetometers "dip sharply". In one example he sent a text alert at exactly 21:28 UT, when the dip began ([glendaleskye.com phases](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-phases.php)). His guidance, as relayed by search, is to **ignore the IMF and Bz predictions once a substorm is in expansion** ([Chasing Aurora Australis](https://chasingaurora.com.au/glendale-app-your-ultimate-guide/)).

**Inferred.** In effect the app has a "driver" layer (solar wind measured at L1 and shifted to its arrival time at Earth) and a "response" layer (ground disturbance). The substorm index probably resembles the scientific AL index, which tracks the most negative drop in the horizontal magnetic field across high-latitude stations. For UK users it probably favours the Norwegian stations. **Unknown.** We do not know which stations it uses, how it removes the quiet-day baseline, how it combines stations, or what calibration turns nT into "visible from where you are". **Nobody outside the app can rebuild this calibration.** It rests on a decade of Stables's nightly photos from Skye, plus Caroline Weir's observation logs for the Iceland version ([user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)).

## Glendale's IMF screen uses 30-minute windows timed to arrival at Earth

**Documented.** The key sentence, as search returned it, reads: "The table shows an analysis of the interplanetary magnetic field in the **30 minute window that is currently arriving at Earth, with windows either side of it**. The 'percentage' figure indicates how negative the Bz was, the ideal is 100% negative" ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/)). The chart shows "Bt … at the top, Bz in the middle and the optimum Bz at the bottom (grey)", and you want Bz "as close to the lower grey line as possible". Theta is "the clock angle (θ) over the last 24 hours, which allows you to see the periods when the IMF was best aligned". The app frames the IMF as a forecast: "We use it to predict activity 1 to 3 hours from now", because "a well-aligned IMF arriving at Earth NOW does NOT result in aurora until 1 to 3 hours later" ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/); [user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)). The advanced FAQ says the only inputs he needs for substorms are "the IMF Bt and Bz at Earth". It criticises standard coupling formulas because their clock-angle term has no hysteresis and can rise with By while Bz is positive, "even though no loading is occurring" ([faqs-advanced.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/faqs-advanced.php)).

A third-party guide (not the developer) adds three practical points. When **both the Now and Next rows are "lit"**, a substorm is underway or likely within about an hour. If Next is not lit, activity may fade. And the window duration **"varies based on Solar wind speed"** ([Chasing Aurora Australis](https://chasingaurora.com.au/glendale-app-your-ultimate-guide/)).

**How the propagation delay works.** NOAA's `propagated-solar-wind` feed records each 1-minute L1 measurement with a `propagated_time_tag`. SWPC calculates that tag with **simple ballistic (flat-front) propagation to 32 Earth radii upstream**. That point is the edge of its Geospace model, not the ground ([SWPC Geospace plot](https://www.spaceweather.gov/products/geospace-geomagnetic-activity-plot); [Cash et al. 2016](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_134080)). In one logged example the tag ran 38 minutes after the L1 time at 486 km/s ([clemensv/real-time-sources](https://github.com/clemensv/real-time-sources/tree/main/feeders/noaa-swpc-l1)). **Errors of ±15 minutes are common**, and NOAA's tests found that a tilted-front method did no better in real-time use ([Cash et al. 2016](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_134080)). That error is a good reason for 30-minute windows: anything finer than about 10–15 minutes claims more timing precision than the data have.

**Inferred.** The third-party remark that window length "varies with speed" probably reflects how much future data exists. Under a simple ballistic estimate the delay is about 54 minutes at 400 km/s but only about 27 minutes at 800 km/s. In fast wind, a 30-minute Next window therefore runs out of data before it ends. One snippet from the user guide says other apps' IMF figures "lag by about 2 to 2.5 hours" ([user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)). That figure does not fit a 30–60 minute transit time. It may be a garbled paraphrase that combines transit time with the 1–3 hour loading time, so do not build on it until you have read the page.

## Alerts fire on measured ground disturbance, gated by darkness and location

**Documented.** Basic push alerts begin with **"onset"** and then escalate through **yellow, amber, red, major, severe and extreme**. They arrive even when the app is closed ([user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)). Optional "activity alerts" fire much more often, but only while the app is open. They cover "all thresholds being crossed", user sighting reports and photo uploads. A desktop "Expansion Alarm" beeps when a substorm enters its expansion phase ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/); [glendaleskye.com](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-alerts-app.php)). Alerts go out only when activity is strong enough "to be photographed in your country" and when "it is dark enough for the aurora to be seen" ([glendaleskye.com](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-alerts-app.php)). "The threshold at which aurora becomes visible for you will be lower the closer to the auroral zone you are." Northern Scotland reaches the lower levels first, and **red applies to the whole UK and Ireland** ([aurora-alerts.uk](https://aurora-alerts.uk/); [user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)). Telegram posts read like "ORANGE ALERT: Moderate geomagnetic substorm in progress" ([Telegram](https://t.me/s/GlendaleApp)).

Location is read "once each time you start the app running, when you report a sighting and when you save your details". It is used to time darkness and to estimate "how far away from the aurora you are" ([user-guide.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/user-guide.php)). The app switches country mode automatically when you arrive somewhere new, and it names three essentials: **geomagnetic activity, clear sky and darkness** ([faqs.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/faqs.php)).

**Inferred.** The alerts appear to fire on the magnetometer index (an onset or expansion detection plus level crossings), not on the IMF screen. **No source says that the IMF percentage or theta triggers a push alert.** Because they react to measured ground disturbance, the alerts mean "it's happening now". The 21:28 UT example shows an alert sent at the moment of the dip ([glendaleskye.com phases](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-phases.php)). Lead time comes from the IMF screen, which the user reads for themselves. **No evidence of a single combined score was found.** A colour-coded IMF rating ("simple, colour-coded indications of how good the Interplanetary Magnetic Field is looking") and the tiered alerts do that job between them ([glendaleskye.com](https://www.glendaleskye.com/aurora-alerts-app.php)).

**Unknown.** The nT value for each tier, the onset-detection rule, cooldowns, the distance-to-visibility formula, the darkness definition (civil, nautical or astronomical twilight), whether cloud cover is used, and how often the IMF panel refreshes. Independent data on false alarms or lead times also does not exist. User praise is anecdotal, apart from one report of Glendale and AuroraWatch UK alerting "within minutes of each other" ([Duluth News Tribune](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/lifestyle/astro-bob/astro-bob-whats-the-best-aurora-app)).

## Replicating it in Aurora Dash: shorten the windows, then add a ground check

**Step 1: change the windows.** Aurora Dash's current layout (Now = the last 60 minutes, Past = 60–120 minutes ago) uses windows twice as long as Glendale's, and it treats "Now" as already arrived. The closest match to the documented "30-minute window currently arriving, with windows either side" is to select rows by `propagated_time_tag`. Next would be (now, now+30], Now would be (now−30, now] and Past would be (now−60, now−30]. Whether Glendale centres Now on the present (now−15 to now+15) is unknown. Make the window length and anchor a setting, so you can line Aurora Dash up with Glendale on a live night. Show a small "minutes of data" count under Next. At high speed the feed holds less than 30 minutes of future, which is the honest version of "window length varies with speed". If you want "arrival at the ground" rather than "arrival at 32 Earth radii", you could add a further 5–10 minutes, but that figure is a rough estimate that no source confirms.

**Step 2: fetch enough history.** The 1-hour propagated file holds only the last hour of L1 measurements. With a 40-minute delay, their arrival times run from about 20 minutes ago to 40 minutes ahead, which is not enough for a Past window or a 24-hour theta plot. Use the 7-day `products/geospace/propagated-solar-wind.json` (about 10,080 one-minute rows, header `time_tag, speed, density, temperature, bx, by, bz, bt, vx, vy, vz, propagated_time_tag`) and trim it on the device ([clemensv/real-time-sources](https://github.com/clemensv/real-time-sources/tree/main/feeders/noaa-swpc-l1)). Check your other endpoints as well. The old `products/solar-wind/mag-*.json` and `plasma-*.json` URLs reportedly return 404 ([HotEARTH](https://github.com/Hotragn/HotEARTH)). **SWPC stopped using DSCOVR on 30 June 2026. SOLAR-1 is now the primary spacecraft, with ACE as backup** ([SWPC news](https://www.spaceweather.gov/news/solar-wind-data-and-display-changes)). If you also read `json/rtsw/rtsw_mag_1m.json`, keep only records marked `active`; they carry `bx_gsm, by_gsm, bz_gsm, bt` ([indi-allsky](https://github.com/aaronwmorris/indi-allsky); [smittix/intercept](https://github.com/smittix/intercept)). Skip null rows, and count them so a data gap does not look like 0% negative.

**Step 3: keep your metrics, and add the grey line.** Your percent negative (the share of valid minutes with Bz < 0) is the most likely reading of Glendale's figure. To test the other reading, also compute a weighted version, such as the average of max(0, −Bz)/Bt, and compare both against Glendale's numbers. For theta, check that a due-south field reads 180°, which is the standard clock-angle convention ([BoM](https://www.sws.bom.gov.au/Category/Solar/Solar%20Conditions/Solar%20Wind%20Clock%20Angle/Solar%20Wind%20Clock%20Angle.php)). Plot −Bt as a dashed "best possible Bz" line under your Bz trace. Following the developer's own warning about By, **do not let theta make a window look good while Bz is positive** ([faqs-advanced.php](https://aurora-alerts.uk/faqs-advanced.php)).

**Step 4: add a "lit" rule and an IMF alert.** No threshold is published, so these are suggested starting points to tune, not Glendale's values. Mark a window "lit" when at least 70% of its minutes are negative and its average Bz is at or below about −3 nT. Send an **"IMF loading" heads-up when Now and Next are both lit**, which follows the third-party "both rows lit" reading. Add a stronger tier for the popular rule of thumb of Bz at −10 nT or lower for 30 minutes ([Space Weather Tracker](https://www-spaceweathertracker-com.lovable.app/imf-bz)). Physics supports a loading window of this length: classic substorms follow a median of about 64–72 minutes of southward field ([Lyons et al. 1997](https://wind.nasa.gov/imp8/abstracts/97-Lyons.htm)). Build in the hysteresis the developer says the textbook formulas lack. A condition must hold for several minutes before an alert fires, and it clears only below a lower bar. Add a cooldown so a flickering Bz cannot spam a driver on Android Auto.

**Step 5: add a public stand-in for the ground layer.** You cannot copy Glendale's substorm index, but three public substitutes exist. The first is NOAA's **1-minute estimated Kp** (`json/planetary_k_index_1m.json`, field `estimated_kp`); smooth it over about 15 minutes before setting thresholds ([meshcore-bot](https://github.com/agessaman/meshcore-bot)). The second is **OVATION** (`json/ovation_aurora_latest.json`), a grid of visible-aurora probabilities updated every 5 minutes. Read the cell nearest the user, or the highest value a few degrees poleward ([SWPC](https://www.spaceweather.gov/products/aurora-30-minute-forecast); [Cartopy](https://cartopy.readthedocs.io/stable/gallery/scalar_data/aurora_forecast.html)). For UK users the third is **AuroraWatch UK's** magnetometer levels. Only its 200 nT red threshold is confirmed by its own blog ([AuroraWatch UK](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/aurorawatchuk/page/4/)). If you later get a 1-minute feed from a high-latitude station such as Tromsø (feed access was not researched), the standard onset test is a drop of more than 45 nT within 3 minutes, followed by an average more than 100 nT below the starting value over the next 26 minutes ([Hajra et al.](https://birkeland.uib.no/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/130_Hajra_JGR_NOA.pdf)). Send a provisional alert on the drop and confirm it later. Gate every alert on darkness, using the sun's elevation at the phone's location; you choose the twilight level, because Glendale's is unknown.

**Step 6: validate.** On an active night, log Aurora Dash's three windows each minute next to screenshots of Glendale's table. Matching percentages will settle the window anchor, the window length and the percent formula better than any further desk research.

## Conclusion

The most useful finding is that Glendale's reputation rests mostly on its **ground-truth layer, not on clever IMF maths**. Its IMF screen is close to what Aurora Dash already computes: simple statistics over short windows timed to arrival at Earth, read as a 1–3 hour heads-up. Its edge comes from watching Norwegian magnetometers in real time and from years of calibration against photos taken on Skye. Aurora Dash can match the IMF screen almost exactly after a day's work on windows and feeds. It cannot match the alert quality without a ground signal, and any public substitute (estimated Kp, OVATION or a single-station onset test) will be coarser and less tuned to your location.

That suggests an honest design goal. Aurora Dash should not be presented as a Glendale clone. Make it a clear "loading" gauge (are Now and Next both southward?) with a separate "is it happening?" light fed by public ground data. Then calibrate both against your own sightings, which is how Stables built his.
